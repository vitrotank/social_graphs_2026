01 / THE SECOND DISPATCH

WEEK 2 · MODELS & NULL MODELS

Does Marvel look
scale-free, random,
or neither?

When physicists opened Wikipedia's hood, they saw power laws everywhere. But does Marvel’s actual degree distribution follow a Barabási–Albert scale-free process, an Erdős–Rényi random baseline, or something else entirely?

We computed the Complementary Cumulative Distribution Function (CCDF), P(K ≥ k), for all 303 Marvel character pages. Unlike noisy histogram probability densities, the CCDF aggregates the tail smoothly without artificial binning bias.

The visual verdict is striking: Marvel tracks the Barabási–Albert preferential attachment curve cleanly from k = 2 up to k ≈ 35 with an implied power-law exponent of γ̂ ≈ 2.35 (fitted slope −1.35 in log–log space). Meanwhile, it bears zero resemblance to the Erdős–Rényi random network, which plummets vertically like a stone at k = 8.

SCIENTIFIC RIGOR NOTE

“An R² of 0.98 on log–log axes proves a power law.”

The ultimate exam trap.
A straight line is not proof.

↳ FIELD DICTIONARY

CCDF = P(K ≥ k), fraction of nodes with degree at least k.
Finite-Size Cutoff = Tail breakdown caused by running out of characters (N = 303).

What you CANNOT conclude from this fit

It is tempting to declare Marvel a "scale-free network." That claim would fail any rigorous peer review. Here is why:

  • The Finite-Size Truncation: In a network of only 303 nodes, each character represents a discrete quantum of probability (ΔP ≈ 0.0041). Past k = 33, only five heroes exist: Deadpool (33), Doctor Strange (50), Wolverine (60), Hulk (64), and Spider-Man (106). The tail does not stretch across three orders of magnitude—it runs out of superheroes.
  • OLS Linear Regression is Statistically Invalid: Fitting a least-squares line to log-transformed cumulative data violates the assumption of independent errors. Cumulative bins are autocorrelated by definition, which artificially inflates R² even on data drawn from lognormal or stretched exponential distributions.
  • Inconclusive Model Selection: Under the gold-standard Clauset–Shalizi–Newman (2009) framework, distinguishing a pure power law from a truncated power law or a lognormal distribution requires Vuong's likelihood-ratio test. At N = 303, the test has insufficient statistical power to reject the lognormal alternative (p > 0.05).

The robust verdict: The Marvel network is definitively heavy-tailed and non-random (p < 10−15 against Erdős–Rényi), but claiming it follows an exact scale-free power law is unsupported by sample size.

↳ THE "BEST WRONG SENTENCE"

“Because the empirical CCDF follows a clear straight line with R² = 0.98, this rigorously proves the network is scale-free.”

Brought to class from our LLM audit: confident, plausible, and mathematically completely wrong.

03 / THE POPULARITY TRAP

THE FRIENDSHIP PARADOX IN THE COMIC UNIVERSE

Why your comic friends
have more friends than you.

In 1991, sociologist Scott Feld noticed a persistent psychological curse: on average, your friends have more friends than you do. In Marvel's universe, this is not an insecurity—it is an algebraic certainty.

〈k〉 = 10.26 links

If you pick a Marvel character uniformly at random from the giant component, they connect to an average of about 10 other heroes.

B / THE AVERAGE FRIEND

〈k_friend〉 = 22.25 links

22.3 friend's average degree

If you pick a random superhero edge and follow it to a neighbor, that friend has over 2.1× more connections on average!

C / THE PARADOX VICTIMS

87.4% Trapped

250 out of 286 heroes

For nearly nine out of ten characters, the average degree of their friends strictly exceeds their own degree.

The One-Line Proof & The Inspection Paradox

Why does this happen? The secret lies in how edges sample nodes. When you average over people, every superhero gets one vote. But when you average over friends, you sample nodes proportionally to their degree. A hero with 100 links appears on 100 friend lists; a hero with 1 link appears on only one.

Using the excess degree distribution q(k) = k P(k) / 〈k〉 and the definition of variance σ² = 〈k²〉 − 〈k〉²:

# The exact mathematical equality:
〈k_friend〉 = Σ k · q(k) = (1 / 〈k〉) Σ k² P(k) = 〈k²〉 / 〈k〉
            = (〈k〉² + σ²) / 〈k〉
            = 〈k〉 + (σ² / 〈k〉)

Because variance σ² ≥ 0, 〈kfriend〉 ≥ 〈k〉 for any graph. The paradox vanishes if and only if σ² = 0—a perfectly regular lattice where everyone has identical degree.

Who drives it? The mega-hubs. Connecting to Spider-Man (106), Wolverine (65), or Captain America (57) pulls up the average of dozens of minor characters.

Is there a character nobody is out-popularityed by?
Yes! In the entire 277-node giant component, Spider-Man is the single undefeated character: his degree (106) strictly exceeds every single one of his 106 neighbors (his highest-degree neighbor is Wolverine at 65). Across the entire universe, only one other character holds local supremacy: Radian (Morituri), who rules the isolated nine-node Morituri island with degree 6 (max neighbor degree 3).

↳ DISASSORTATIVE BOOST

Marvel's degree assortativity is r = −0.105 (disassortative). Hubs link preferentially to peripheral heroes, ensuring almost everyone has a superstar friend to make them feel unpopular!

The Mystery of the Two Roads: 0.11 vs 0.155

When generating a degree-preserving null model, two standard algorithms exist: Stub Matching (the Configuration Model) and Double Edge Swapping (the Maslov–Sneppen Markov chain). In theory, both sample graphs with the exact same degree sequence. Yet in our simulations, stub matching produced an average clustering of C ≈ 0.11, while double edge swaps produced C ≈ 0.155. Where did the difference come from?

The Culprit: Pruning Multi-Edges Between Hubs.
Stub matching creates multigraphs. The probability of forming a self-loop or parallel link between nodes i and j is proportional to ki kj / (2m). In heavy-tailed networks, this collision probability is astronomically high between the mega-hubs (Spider-Man, Wolverine, Iron Man, Captain America). Converting the multigraph into a simple graph threw away an average of 102.7 edges (over 7.2% of all edges!). Because these dropped edges connected the hubs, discarding them obliterated the dense core of triangles that drives clustering (C ∝ 〈k²〉² / 〈k〉³).

Double edge swaps work directly in the space of simple graphs, losing zero edges. It is the true, unbiased null model for simple networks.

THE TAKEAWAY

Degree sequence alone quadruples clustering compared to pure chance (from 0.037 to 0.155). But Marvel's real world (0.320) doubles that again, proving superhero cliques are real.

05 / GROW YOUR OWN MARVEL

PREFERENTIAL ATTACHMENT AT N = 303

What happens when you
simulate the universe?

We grew a synthetic universe using Barabási–Albert preferential attachment, tuned exactly to Marvel's parameters: N = 303 nodes, with each newcomer attaching to m = 6 existing characters (≈ 1,784 / 303). Here is what the model got right—and what it got glaringly wrong.

01 / WHAT BA GETS RIGHT

Hubs & Scale

The "rich-get-richer" mechanism naturally creates superstar hubs and a heavy-tailed degree distribution spanning multiple orders of magnitude, accompanied by compact path lengths (〈d〉 ≈ 2.6).

02 / WHAT BA GETS WRONG

Camaraderie & Cliques

Clustering in the BA model is near zero (C ≈ 0.041), completely missing Marvel’s tight-knit superhero teams (C = 0.320). BA has no mechanism for triadic closure.

The Most Glaring Flaws of Barabási–Albert:

  1. Zero Isolates or Islands: In BA, every newcomer connects to m nodes, guaranteeing that the graph is connected into a single component. It cannot produce the 17 isolated heroes or the 9-node Morituri island.
  2. Strict First-Mover Determinism: In standard BA, node degree is purely a function of node age (Πi ∝ ki). The oldest characters are irrevocably the biggest hubs. In actual comic publishing, newer heroes (e.g. Deadpool, introduced in 1991) rapidly eclipsed hundreds of characters created in the 1940s and 1960s. Real networks require fitness and cultural relevance, not just seniority!
↳ FITNESS VS AGE

The Bianconi–Barabási model introduces character fitness: even late arrivals can attract links if their cultural appeal is high enough. Standard BA lacks this entirely.

PUBLICATION SUMMARY (DTU 02805)
“To test whether the high clustering of the Marvel co-appearance network (C = 0.320) reflects genuine triadic closure or is merely an artifact of degree heterogeneity, we compared it against a degree-preserving randomized null model generated via double-edge swapping (Cnull = 0.155 ± 0.008). While the heavy-tailed degree sequence mechanically quadruples clustering relative to a naive Erdős–Rényi baseline (CER = 0.037 ± 0.003), the empirical network still exhibits more than double the clustering expected by degree sequence alone (z = 19.6, p < 0.001). We therefore conclude that thematic modularity—such as superhero teams and shared comic storylines—drives substantial local cohesiveness that cannot be explained by hub connectivity alone.”

01 / REPRODUCIBLE RECIPE

Shuffle with double-edge swaps.

# The unbiased simple-graph null:
import networkx as nx

# Load giant connected component
gcc = max(nx.connected_components(G), key=len)
H = G.subgraph(gcc).copy()

# Double edge swaps preserve simple graph
nx.double_edge_swap(H, nswap=10 * H.number_of_edges(), max_tries=100000)
c_null = nx.average_clustering(H)

Never drop edges from stub matching without accounting for hub-hub link decimation.
